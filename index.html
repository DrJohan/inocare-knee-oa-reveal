A plain-language guide

Understanding knee osteoarthritis

What an X-ray grade means—and what knee ultrasound can show

For patients and families

Introduce the presentation as general education. Osteoarthritis is assessed by a clinician; images are only one part of the picture. Sources: WHO osteoarthritis fact sheet — https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/osteoarthritis NICE NG226 — https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng226/chapter/Recommendations Kohn MD et al. 2016 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4925407/ D'Agostino V et al. 2024 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11355885/

What is knee osteoarthritis?

It is a long-term condition that can affect the whole knee joint.

Cartilage

The smooth covering may become thinner or less even.

Joint lining

The lining may become irritated, thickened or swollen.

Knee
joint

Bone

The bone can form small extra edges called bone spurs.

Muscles and support tissues

Weakness or altered support can affect movement.

It is more than simple “wear and tear”, and it is not an unavoidable part of ageing.

Osteoarthritis affects cartilage, bone, the joint lining and surrounding tissues. Avoid implying that the knee is simply worn out. Sources: WHO — https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/osteoarthritis OARSI — https://oarsi.org/what-osteoarthritis

How might it feel in everyday life?

Symptoms vary. A person may have some of these, not all of them.

Move

Pain with activity

Walking, stairs, standing up or longer periods on your feet may hurt.

Start

Stiff after rest

The knee may feel slow or tight when you first get moving.

Swell

Swelling or fullness

The knee may look or feel puffy, warm or tight.

Do

Less movement or confidence

Daily tasks, exercise or sleep may become more difficult.

Pain is personal: two people with similar scans can feel very different.

Symptoms include pain, swelling, stiffness and difficulty moving. Scan severity and pain do not match perfectly between individuals. Sources: WHO — https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/osteoarthritis Bedson and Croft 2008 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2542996/

A scan is not always needed to diagnose osteoarthritis

For a typical pattern, clinicians usually start with your story and an examination.

1

Your story

Where it hurts, what brings it on, stiffness, swelling and how it affects daily life.

2

Examination

Movement, tenderness, swelling, strength and how you walk or use the knee.

3

When a scan helps

An X-ray or ultrasound may answer a specific question or check another cause.

A common clinical pattern

Age 45 or olderPain linked to activityNo morning stiffness, or 30 minutes or less

Do not present this as a self-diagnosis checklist. NICE recommends clinical diagnosis without routine imaging for adults aged 45 or over with activity-related pain and no morning stiffness, or stiffness lasting 30 minutes or less. Sources: NICE NG226 recommendations 1.1.1–1.1.2 — https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng226/chapter/Recommendations

X-ray and ultrasound show different parts of the picture

X-ray

Shows bones, bone spurs and the space between the bones. The KL grade comes from an X-ray.

Ultrasound

Shows moving images of accessible soft tissues, fluid and the joint lining. It uses sound waves, not radiation.

Neither scan measures pain.

X-ray assesses radiographic bone and joint-space changes. Ultrasound gives real-time information about accessible soft tissue and fluid. Neither image is a pain score. Sources: NICE NG226 — https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng226/chapter/Recommendations Kohn MD et al. — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4925407/ ESSR knee ultrasound guidance — https://essr.org/content-essr/uploads/2016/10/knee.pdf D'Agostino V et al. — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11355885/

“KL grade” is a 0-to-4 X-ray scale

KL stands for Kellgren–Lawrence. It describes visible structural change.

0

No visible OA

No osteoarthritis change seen on the X-ray.

1

Uncertain

Very small or doubtful change.

2

Mild

A definite bone spur; the joint space may start to narrow.

3

Moderate

Clear narrowing, more bone spurs and denser bone.

4

Severe

Marked narrowing and major bony change.

Important: the KL grade is not your pain score and should not be used alone to decide treatment.

The Kellgren–Lawrence classification is a five-grade radiographic scale. Published descriptions vary slightly. It is a structural X-ray scale, not a symptom score or stand-alone treatment rule. Sources: Kohn MD et al. — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4925407/ NICE NG226 recommendation 1.6.2 — https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng226/chapter/Recommendations Bedson and Croft — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2542996/

What happens during a knee ultrasound?

1

Gel and probe

A clinician places gel on the skin and moves a small probe around the knee.

2

Moving pictures

Sound waves create real-time images. You may be asked to bend or straighten the knee.

3

Focused questions

The scan can look for extra fluid, an irritated lining, bone spurs and accessible soft tissues.

No ionising radiation—but ultrasound cannot see through bone or show every part of the joint.

Ultrasound is real-time and uses no ionising radiation. Image quality and interpretation depend on technique and operator; bone blocks the sound beam, so the entire joint is not visible. Sources: ESSR — https://essr.org/content-essr/uploads/2016/10/knee.pdf D'Agostino V et al. — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11355885/

What can look normal on knee ultrasound?

Smooth cartilage

A regular dark band over a bright bone surface.

Regular bone edge

The visible outer bone line looks smooth and continuous.

Organised tendons

Tendon fibres look even and run in the same direction.

No extra fluid

The joint spaces and small sacs are not abnormally stretched.

“Normal” depends on the right probe position, image angle and gentle pressure.

The displayed example is accessible trochlear cartilage. A trained clinician interprets the whole examination rather than one still image. Sources: ESSR — https://essr.org/content-essr/uploads/2016/10/knee.pdf D'Agostino V et al., Figure 6A — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11355885/

Abnormal finding: extra fluid or an irritated lining

Effusion = extra fluid

Fluid can collect inside the joint and may feel like swelling or fullness.

Synovitis = irritated joint lining

A thickened lining may show extra blood flow as colour on Doppler.

Effusion means extra joint fluid. Synovitis means thickening or irritation of the joint lining. Doppler colour can show increased blood flow. These findings can occur with osteoarthritis and other conditions. Sources: Bruyn GA et al. — https://www.jrheum.org/content/early/2019/01/23/jrheum.181095 D'Agostino V et al., Figures 2–3 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11355885/

Other changes ultrasound may find around an arthritic knee

Bone spurs

Extra bony edges at the margin of the joint.

Cartilage change

Accessible cartilage may look rough, uneven or thinner.

Baker cyst

A fluid-filled swelling at the back of the knee.

A finding on ultrasound does not automatically explain all of your pain.

Explain osteophytes as bone spurs and a Baker cyst as fluid distension at the back of the knee. Cartilage can only be assessed in accessible windows. Sources: D'Agostino V et al., Figures 4–6 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11355885/ ESSR — https://essr.org/content-essr/uploads/2016/10/knee.pdf Bedson and Croft — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2542996/

Three useful questions to ask about your scan

A good report makes more sense when it is linked to your symptoms and examination.

1

What did the scan find?

Ask for unfamiliar words to be explained in plain language.

2

Does it match my symptoms?

The same-looking scan can feel different from one person to another.

3

What should I do next?

Discuss activity, exercise, pain support and follow-up based on your needs—not the image alone.

Seek individual advice from a qualified healthcare professional who has assessed you.

Encourage questions and shared decision-making. Imaging should answer a clinical question and should not be used in isolation. Sources: NICE NG226 — https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng226/chapter/Recommendations Bedson and Croft — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2542996/

The simple way to remember it

Three takeaways

1

Osteoarthritis affects the whole knee

It can involve cartilage, bone, the joint lining and the tissues that support movement.

2

KL is an X-ray grade—not a pain score

Grades 0 to 4 describe increasing visible structural change.

3

Ultrasound adds focused information

It can show accessible soft tissues, fluid, movement and blood flow—but not the entire joint.

General education only. Your own symptoms, examination and clinical advice matter most.
